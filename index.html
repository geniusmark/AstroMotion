Astronomical Observatory Guide

Why Do We Have Seasons?

A common misconception is that seasons are caused by Earth moving closer to or farther from the Sun. In reality, Earth's orbit is nearly circular (eccentricity 0.0167), and Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January!

Seasons are caused entirely by Earth's axial tilt of 23.44° relative to the perpendicular of its orbital (ecliptic) plane.

Key Mechanism: As Earth orbits the Sun, its rotational axis remains fixed in direction in inertial space, pointing continuously toward Polaris (the North Star).
  • June Solstice (Summer in North): The Northern hemisphere tilts directly toward the Sun, resulting in steep solar incidence angles, concentrated heat energy, and long daylight hours (Midnight Sun in the Arctic).
  • December Solstice (Winter in North): The Northern hemisphere tilts away, receiving shallow, spread-out sunlight and short daylight hours.
  • Equinoxes (March & September): Neither pole tilts toward the Sun; sunlight strikes the Equator head-on, giving equal 12-hour day and night worldwide.

The Lunar Phase Cycle & Tidal Locking

The Moon does not emit its own visible light; it reflects sunlight. As the Moon circles Earth every 27.3 days (sidereal month), the angle between Sun, Earth, and Moon changes.

Because Earth is also moving around the Sun, it takes the Moon 29.5 days (synodic month) to cycle through all 8 lunar phases from one New Moon to the next.

Tidal Locking: Notice in the simulation how the exact same lunar surface features (such as Mare Tranquillitatis and the Tycho crater rays) always point directly toward Earth! Gravitational tidal friction slowed the Moon's rotation over billions of years until its axial rotation period exactly equaled its orbital period.
  • New Moon: Moon is between Earth and Sun; the illuminated side faces away from Earth.
  • First / Third Quarter: Half of the visible lunar disc is illuminated.
  • Full Moon: Moon is opposite the Sun; the entire near side is bathed in direct sunlight.

Why Don't Eclipses Happen Every Month?

If the Moon orbited in the exact same plane as Earth orbits the Sun (the ecliptic plane), we would have a Solar Eclipse every New Moon and a Lunar Eclipse every Full Moon.

The 5.145° Inclination: The Moon's orbital plane is tilted by approximately 5.145° with respect to the ecliptic plane. Most months, the Moon passes slightly above or below the Sun-Earth line, casting its shadow harmlessly into empty space.

Eclipses can only occur when the Moon crosses the ecliptic at the Line of Nodes (the ascending or descending node) at the exact time of a New or Full Moon:

  • Solar Eclipse: Moon crosses a node during a New Moon, casting its umbral shadow cone onto Earth.
  • Lunar Eclipse (Blood Moon): Moon crosses a node during a Full Moon, entering Earth's umbral shadow cone. Earth's atmosphere filters blue light and refracts sunset-red light onto the lunar surface!
